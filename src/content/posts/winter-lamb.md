I watched *The Sheep Detectives* recently, a movie that is exactly what it sounds like: sheep, solving crimes, being dramatic about it, and I love it! Lots of "mehhhh", lots of very committed CGI sheep, lots of sheep running (yeah, they do run and fast!). But this post is not about the movie, actually. In the movie, there's a plot point about a "winter lamb", a lamb born in winter, an outcast. Shunned by the flock. Viewed with suspicion. A whole social hierarchy based on what month your mom decided to have you, well, based on what month your mother and father, well, you know what I mean.

I walked away from that movie slightly emotionally affected (only slightly!) and also mildly curious: is that a real thing? Are winter lambs actually treated differently?

So I went looking (again!). And the answer is: no (fortunately), the movie just made that up (who would have thought?). But the real story is somehow better.

## Winter lambs are actually fine

Let's get this out of the way: real sheep do not shun lambs for being born in winter. There is no flock politics, no president, no mayor, and left and right are just directions, period! No one is getting voted off the pasture for arriving in December.

Here's the actual situation: winter-born lambs tend to be *healthier* than their spring-born siblings. Fewer parasites is the main reason. Parasitic worms don't particularly enjoy the cold (who would, right?), so a lamb taking its first wobbly steps in January is doing so in a much less worm-infested environment than one born in April. The supposed outcast is thriving. The movie had it completely backwards.

## Okay but is it even possible for lamb to be born in winter?

Yes! And this is where it gets interesting.

Sheep are what's called "short-day breeders". The shortening of autumn days triggers their mating season. This is the sheep's built-in mechanism of ensuring lambs arrive in spring, when there's food, warmth, and grass. A ewe (female sheep)'s pregnancy is about five months, so: mating in November → lambs in April. That's the default setting. That's what sheep do left to their own devices.

But biology is not the boss of everyone. Some breeds naturally cycle differently and will lamb (giving birth to lambs) in winter without any intervention. Nobody told them the rules and they simply chose chaos (you go, sheep!). For others, farmers can adjust light exposure to nudge their biology into an earlier cycle. Trick the sheep into thinking autumn came early, and the rest follows.

So yes, winter lambs exist, they have always existed, and some sheep are out there doing it completely on purpose, while some are tricked and some just don't care about rules, I guess.

## The real vocabulary is even better

Here's the part I didn't expect when I started googling this.

Real sheep farmers don't just say a sheep is "one year old" or "two years old" when someone asks them how old their sheep are (and I imagine they get asked that a lot!). They measure age in *winters survived*. A sheep that has lived through two winters is called a **twinter**, literally a contraction of "two winters" (very creative indeed!). A sheep that has survived three winters is a **thrunter**, I don't know, a contraction of "three survived winters"? (Actually, it's a contraction of three and winter, and no one knows where the *u* came from. And in case you're wondering what about a lamb that survived one winter, don't they also deserve the recognition? Of course, they do! A one-winter lamb is called a hogg or hogget or shearling or teg. I guess that's double, no, quadruple amount of respect!)

This is not a quirky historical thing, it's a deeply practical way of thinking. Surviving winter is the hardest part. A sheep that made it through two winters has proven it can handle scarce food, freezing temperatures, and disease. It has demonstrated its value. Age in winters is age in *resilience*, not just time passing.

Compare that to "two years old," which is just a solar orbit counter. Completely abstract. Tells you nothing about the animal. Twinter tells you everything.

We measure human age in years and honestly we might be missing something. So maybe instead of "I'm 27 years old", we should say, "I've survived 27 winters." Respect earned! (At least with the sheep)

## The movie still got something right

The filmmakers took the real logic, which is measuring a sheep's life by winters, and flipped it into a social status system. Instead of "how many winters have you survived," the movie asks "were you *born in winter*?" It's the same word, completely different meaning, used to make a point about how groups invent arbitrary rules to decide who belongs. (This is just what I think, at least)

Which is very clever. And also completely made up. And also very bleak, like don't we have enough problems already, stop inventing new ones! 

Real winter lambs are healthy, unbothered, and nobody is shunning them.

What's the real learning here? Do not trust what movies tell you! Or at least, google it or read about it here!

And now, ask someone to watch *The Sheep Detectives* with you so you can explain to them during the movie what a winter lamb is. Or what a twinter is.
